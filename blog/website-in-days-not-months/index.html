How I Design and Ship a Website in Days, Not Months

The old timeline wasn't craft. It was queueing.

Short answer: A focused marketing site takes about a week — roughly one day to align on scope, two to three days to see real design directions, two to three days to build and hand-finish, and a day to launch. That's not a corner-cut version of the eight-week process. Most of those eight weeks were never craft; they were queueing, approval gaps, and production grind that AI now absorbs.

Here's the whole thing, honestly, including where it goes wrong.

Why the old timeline was long

Break down a traditional eight-week web project and you find maybe ten working days of actual design and development. The rest is structural:

  • Waiting for your project to reach the top of someone's queue
  • Waiting on internal reviews between an account manager and a designer
  • Mockup rounds where each iteration costs half a day of manual production
  • Waiting on copy that nobody was clearly responsible for
  • Handoff friction between whoever designed it and whoever builds it

Almost none of that made the work better. Three of those five disappear when you work directly with the person doing the work, and the other two shrink dramatically when iteration stops being expensive.

Day 1 — Align

One call, usually 30 to 45 minutes. Your market, your competitors, who you're trying to reach, what you want them to do, and — the part most briefs skip — your taste. I'd rather see three sites you love and one you hate than read a positioning document.

You leave with a fixed scope, a fixed timeline, and a flat price, usually the same day. If the project isn't a fit, that's when I say so.

What I need from you: decisiveness, and any brand assets that already exist. If you don't have copy, say so now so it's scoped rather than discovered at 80%.

Days 2–3 — Design

This is where AI changes the shape of the work. Instead of mocking up two directions and defending them, I explore many: layout systems, type pairings, color languages, motion behavior. Concepting that used to be a week of production is a morning of exploration.

Then the important half. Everything generated is a starting point, not a deliverable. I pick a direction, kill the rest, and rebuild it by hand — the type gets sized properly, the spacing gets a system, the copy gets rewritten, the details get decided by a person who knows why. AI makes version one. I make version two.

You see real directions in days, on real content, not lorem ipsum over a template.

What I need from you: one round of specific feedback. "The second one, but calmer, and the hero headline is wrong" is useful. "Not sure, send more options" is how weeks disappear.

Days 4–6 — Build

Hand-finished front-end code. No page builders, no plugin sprawl. AI scaffolds the repetitive structure; I handle interaction, motion, responsive behavior, and the twenty small judgment calls per page that make the difference between "fine" and "expensive."

Baked in from the first line rather than retrofitted:

  • Semantic HTML and a real heading hierarchy
  • Performance discipline — static where possible, minimal JavaScript, disciplined images
  • Accessibility: contrast, focus states, keyboard paths, reduced-motion support
  • SEO and answer-engine structure: metadata, schema, answer-first copy blocks (why that matters now)

You get a live staging link, not a PDF. Click it on your phone at dinner.

Day 7 — Ship & evolve

Domain, DNS, analytics, forms tested, search console and Bing submitted, sitemap live. Then a short walkthrough so you can actually update your own site.

Launch is the start. What you measure in the first month — where people land, where they leave, what they click before emailing — tells you what the second version should be. Some clients take that themselves, some keep me on as an ongoing creative partner.

Where fast projects actually go wrong

Three failure modes, and all three are avoidable:

  1. Copy arrives late. The single most common cause of a one-week project becoming a one-month project. Scope it at day one, either to me or to you with a deadline.
  2. Too many decision-makers. Speed comes from a short approval chain. If four people must agree, the calendar wins.
  3. Scope grows mid-build. "Can we also add a booking system" is a legitimate request and a different project. I'd rather quote it separately than quietly blow the timeline.

Isn't fast just worse?

It's the fair question, so: fast is worse when speed comes from templating, from skipping strategy, or from letting a generator make the final call. That's the $50 tier, and it's a real tier — here's when you should use it.

Fast is better when the compression comes from eliminating queueing, overhead, and manual production grind while a human keeps every decision that shapes the outcome. You also get a real benefit nobody talks about: when iteration is cheap, you try more things. The version that ships is usually the fifth idea, not the first — which is the opposite of what a long, expensive process produces.

FAQ

How long does it take to build a website?

A focused marketing site or landing page takes roughly a week here. A larger site with brand identity work runs two to four. A traditional agency quotes the same work at eight to twenty weeks, most of which is process rather than production.

Do you use templates?

No. Every build is designed and hand-coded for the specific business. AI accelerates exploration and scaffolding; it doesn't pick the direction and it doesn't ship unedited.

What do you need from me to start?

A couple of sentences about the project, then 45 minutes on a call. Existing brand assets if you have them, and an honest answer about whether you have copy.

What if I don't like the first direction?

One round of specific feedback and we redirect — that's a day, not a week, which is the entire point of working this way.
